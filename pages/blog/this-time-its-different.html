This Time It's Different: The Four Most Expensive Words in Investing

Every bubble in history arrived with a sophisticated argument for why normal valuation rules didn't apply. Every single one was eventually wrong.

The 30-Second Version

If you only have 30 seconds, here is the whole argument. The rest of the post is the receipts.

  • The phrase always sounds credible. Every bubble — dot-com, housing, crypto — is backed by a real innovation or genuine trend. That's what makes the argument so dangerous.
  • The claim is never true in the way that matters. No innovation has ever permanently suspended the relationship between price and underlying value. Asset prices always eventually reconnect with cash flows.
  • The historical toll is staggering. The NASDAQ fell 78% after the dot-com peak and took 15 years to recover. The housing crash erased an estimated $22 trillion in global wealth. Crypto's 2022 collapse destroyed hundreds of billions.
  • Mean reversion always wins eventually. P/E ratios, profit margins, and asset prices that deviate dramatically from historical norms tend to return — just on a timeline that can outlast your patience and your portfolio.
  • VTI doesn't need any story to be "different." The thesis — that human productive activity compounds over decades — has survived every crash, bubble, and crisis the last 100 years produced. That's not a narrative. It's the historical record.

Sir John Templeton — one of the greatest investors of the 20th century — called them "the four most expensive words in the English language." Not "I love you unconditionally" or "I don't need a lawyer." Four words that have cost investors more money than fraud, more than market crashes, more than bad timing: this time it's different.

Every major market bubble in recorded history has been accompanied by a sophisticated, intellectually serious argument for why normal valuation principles don't apply. Every single one. And every single one has eventually been proven wrong by the fundamental economic forces that humans have never successfully suspended.

Not because the innovation wasn't real. Not because the technology wasn't transformative. But because "genuinely transformative innovation" and "justifies any valuation" are two very different claims, and markets in the grip of mania consistently conflate them.

The Historical Record: When "Different" Became "Same"

The Dot-Com Bubble (1995–2000)

The argument was compelling: the internet was going to change everything. Traditional metrics like price-to-earnings ratios were artifacts of the old economy. Companies were valued on "eyeballs" and "first-mover advantage" because revenue and profit were old-economy concepts that didn't apply to the new paradigm.

The internet did change everything. Amazon, Google, and Facebook became some of the largest and most profitable companies in human history. The innovation was real.

The valuations were not.

The NASDAQ Composite rose from roughly 750 in January 1995 to a peak of 5,048 on March 10, 2000 — a 573% increase in five years. The average price-to-sales ratio of companies that IPO'd in 2000 was nearly 49. Pets.com, Webvan, and Boo.com attracted hundreds of millions in investment capital and went to zero within a year.

The crash erased $5 trillion in market capitalization. The NASDAQ fell 78% and didn't return to its March 2000 high until April 2015 — fifteen years later. The investors who believed "this time is different" and invested at the peak waited a decade and a half just to break even.

This time was not different.

The Housing Bubble (2003–2008)

The argument: home prices never decline nationally. Real estate is local — even if some markets correct, broad national declines are unprecedented. Demographics guarantee sustained demand. And the financial instruments created to distribute mortgage risk make the system more stable, not less.

Every one of these claims had a grain of truth. Home prices had not declined nationally since the Great Depression. Demographics did support housing demand. Financial engineering did distribute risk — it distributed it so widely and opaquely that nobody knew where it was.

What was different this time? Nothing. Asset prices that rise faster than the underlying fundamental value eventually correct. Mortgage underwriting standards that allowed people to borrow amounts they couldn't realistically repay eventually produce defaults. Leverage that amplifies gains on the way up amplifies losses on the way down.

The Case-Shiller Home Price Index fell approximately 27% nationally from 2006 to 2012. In the worst markets — Las Vegas, Phoenix, Miami — prices fell 50–60%. The financial crisis that followed cost the global economy an estimated $22 trillion in lost wealth. Major financial institutions failed or required government bailouts. The unemployment rate reached 10%.

This time was not different.

Cryptocurrency (2017–2022)

The argument: Bitcoin is digital gold. Blockchain represents a fundamental transformation of financial infrastructure. Scarcity is mathematically guaranteed. Central bank money printing makes hard assets essential. Decentralized finance removes the inefficiencies of the traditional banking system.

Again: real insights. Blockchain technology has genuine applications. Bitcoin's fixed supply is a real attribute. Central bank money printing is a real concern. Some blockchain applications will create real value.

Dogecoin — a literal meme, created as a joke, with no fixed supply cap and no unique technology — reached a $90 billion market capitalization in May 2021. Celebrities tweeted about it. It appeared on Saturday Night Live. Its creator told the press it was a "hustle."

Bitcoin fell from $69,000 to below $17,000. Dogecoin fell over 90% from its peak. Terra Luna — a stablecoin ecosystem promoted as a revolution in decentralized finance — collapsed entirely in May 2022, destroying approximately $60 billion in value in a week. FTX, a leading crypto exchange run by Sam Bankman-Fried, collapsed in November 2022 after committing massive fraud. Roughly $8 billion in customer funds evaporated.

This time was not different.

Why the Argument Is Always Convincing

The reason "this time it's different" is so expensive is that it's never completely wrong. There's always a real underlying change that gives the argument its initial credibility.

The internet was genuinely transformative. Housing does tend to appreciate over long periods. Cryptocurrency and blockchain do represent real technological innovation. But the argument doesn't just claim that the underlying change is real — it claims that the underlying change permanently suspends the relationship between price and value.

That second claim has never been true. And it never will be, because markets are ultimately driven by cash flows. The value of any investment is the present value of its future cash flows. A company that generates no revenue and has no path to profitability is not worth billions of dollars regardless of how transformative its technology is. A house is not worth paying $500,000 for if it rents for $1,200 per month. An asset that produces no cash flows is worth only what someone else will pay for it — which is a description of a Ponzi scheme, not an investment.

"The brain wants to believe it. The media is incentivized to amplify it."

Robert Shiller's research on "narrative economics" documents how stories spread through populations and influence economic behavior. The "this time it's different" narrative spreads especially rapidly because it is exciting, it offers the prospect of extraordinary returns, and it provides intellectual justification for desires — getting rich quickly — that we already have. This is not a failure of intelligence. It is a feature of human psychology that affects PhDs, professional investors, and Nobel Prize winners alike. The solution is not to become immune to compelling narratives. The solution is to build a system — like a passive index strategy — that removes the need to evaluate them at all.

Mean Reversion: The Financial Law That Laughs at "Different"

The concept that most reliably humbles "this time it's different" arguments is mean reversion — the long-term tendency of most measurable financial metrics to return toward their historical averages.

Price-to-earnings ratios that expand dramatically during bubbles eventually contract. Profit margins that expand beyond historical norms eventually attract competition that compresses them. Asset prices that decouple from underlying fundamental value eventually reconnect.

This is not a law like gravity — it can take longer than any individual investor's patience can sustain. It is a tendency, observable over long periods, with significant exceptions. But it provides a useful framework for evaluating "this time it's different" claims.

When someone argues that current valuations are justified despite being dramatically higher than historical averages, the question to ask is: what has permanently changed about the fundamental economics that justifies a permanently higher valuation level? If the answer is "technology" or "low interest rates" or "strong demand," the follow-up is: has that factor ever been temporary? Has it ever reversed?

Almost always: yes.

The Boring Answer That Wins Every Time

Here is what's genuinely different about a total market index fund investment thesis: it doesn't depend on any particular story being permanently true.

The thesis for VTI isn't "the internet will change everything" or "real estate never goes down" or "Bitcoin is digital gold." The thesis is: "The US economy, across all its companies and sectors, will be larger and more valuable in 30 years than it is today, because productive human activity creates value over time."

This thesis has been tested more rigorously than any speculative narrative. It survived the dot-com crash, the housing crash, two world wars, the Great Depression, the Cold War, multiple recessions, a global pandemic, and every other crisis the 20th and 21st centuries produced. Not because bad things don't happen to the economy — they do — but because the long-term trajectory of human productivity and economic growth has proven resilient across all of them.

The VTI investor isn't making a bet that something specific is "different this time." They're making a bet that something fundamental hasn't changed: that human beings will continue to be productive, that businesses will continue to generate profits, and that those profits will compound over time.

That's not a narrative. It's the historical record.

The Bottom Line

"This time it's different" is the four most expensive words in investing because they have never been true in the sense that matters: no innovation, no demographic trend, no technological revolution has permanently suspended the relationship between price and underlying value. The assets that justified extraordinary valuations on "different this time" grounds have all eventually corrected — sometimes brutally, sometimes over years, always eventually.

The antidote is not cynicism about innovation or technology. Many transformative technologies have created extraordinary investment returns — for patient, diversified investors who owned them through market-weighted index funds, not concentrated speculators who bought at bubble valuations.

The next time you hear that some asset is different — that it doesn't obey the normal rules of valuation, that the old metrics don't apply, that this opportunity is unique in financial history — remember the tulips, the dotcoms, the houses, the coins. Ask what's different about the relationship between price and underlying value, not just about the technology. If you can't answer that clearly, you're probably looking at another version of the same story.
