The Backdoor Roth IRA: A Legal Loophole Every High Earner Should Know

Income too high for direct Roth contributions? The backdoor Roth converts after-tax money into a Roth IRA — here is the step-by-step playbook, the pro-rata landmine to avoid, and the mega version for serious savers.

The 30-Second Version

If you only have 30 seconds, here is the whole argument. The rest of the post is the receipts.

  • The income limit problem: In 2026, Roth IRA contributions phase out above $153K (single) and $242K (married). If you exceed those, the front door is locked.
  • The backdoor solution: Contribute to a Traditional IRA (non-deductible, no income limit), then immediately convert it to a Roth IRA. No income tax owed if you convert before earnings accumulate.
  • The pro-rata landmine: If you have pre-tax IRA money anywhere, the IRS taxes your conversion proportionally — not just the after-tax dollars. Roll old IRA balances into your 401(k) first to clear the slate.
  • The paperwork that matters: File Form 8606 every single year. This establishes your after-tax basis and prevents you from being taxed twice. Keep it forever.
  • The supercharged version: If your 401(k) allows after-tax contributions, the Mega Backdoor Roth can put up to $47,500/year into Roth — six times the standard IRA limit.

Congratulations — you've worked hard, earned well, and found yourself making too much money to contribute to a Roth IRA. The IRS is basically telling you: "You've been too successful for this tax break."

Infuriating? A little. But here's the thing: Congress left a door open. Not the front door — that one's guarded by income limits. But there's a back door, and it's been there since 2010.

The Backdoor Roth IRA isn't a shady scheme or a tax shelter you'll see in Netflix documentaries about offshore accounts. It's a perfectly legal, IRS-acknowledged strategy used by millions of high earners to access Roth benefits regardless of income. The IRS has confirmed it's valid. Tax attorneys write about it openly. Your brokerage will even help you do it.

Why High Earners Get Locked Out — and the Workaround

The Roth IRA income limits for 2026 are:

  • Single filers: Full contribution if MAGI is below $153,000; phases out completely at $168,000
  • Married filing jointly: Full contribution below $242,000; phases out at $252,000

If your income exceeds these thresholds, you can't contribute directly to a Roth IRA. But here's what you can still do:

  1. Contribute to a Traditional IRA — anyone with earned income can do this, regardless of income. (Note: it won't be tax-deductible if you're covered by a workplace retirement plan at this income level, but that's fine — we don't need the deduction.)
  2. Convert that Traditional IRA contribution to a Roth IRA.

That's the backdoor. You go around the income limit by using the Traditional IRA as a pass-through vehicle.

Step-by-Step: The Standard Backdoor Roth

  1. Open a Traditional IRA (if you don't have one) at any brokerage — Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab, etc.
  2. Make a non-deductible contribution. For 2026, the limit is $7,500 ($8,600 if you're 50 or older).
  3. Do not invest the funds yet — leave them in cash or a money market account to avoid any earnings before conversion.
  4. Convert the Traditional IRA to a Roth IRA. This triggers a taxable event — but since you contributed after-tax dollars and there are no earnings, there's nothing to tax.
  5. You now have $7,500 in your Roth IRA, ready to invest in VTI and let it compound tax-free.

The conversion itself is straightforward at most brokerages — it's often a single button click or a quick online form labeled "convert to Roth IRA."

File Form 8606. Every year. Keep it forever.

File IRS Form 8606 with your tax return each year you make a non-deductible Traditional IRA contribution. This establishes your after-tax basis and proves the contribution was already taxed. Without this form, you could end up paying taxes on it again when you withdraw it. The IRS has no other way to know your basis. Keep every Form 8606 you ever file permanently — yes, permanently — because you may need to reference contributions made decades ago when you finally take distributions in retirement.

The Pro-Rata Rule: The Hidden Landmine

The backdoor Roth looks clean in the example above. But there's a catch that bites a lot of people: the pro-rata rule.

Here's how it works: The IRS doesn't let you cherry-pick which IRA dollars you're converting. If you have any pre-tax money in any Traditional IRA, SEP IRA, or SIMPLE IRA, the IRS treats all your IRAs as one big pool. Your conversion is taxed proportionally across the entire balance.

Example of the pro-rata problem:

You have a rollover IRA with $93,000 in pre-tax dollars (from an old 401(k)), and you add a new $7,000 non-deductible contribution to a separate Traditional IRA. Total IRA balance: $100,000. Only $7,000 (7%) is after-tax.

When you convert that $7,000, only 7% is tax-free ($490). The remaining 93% ($6,510) is taxable — at ordinary income rates. Your clean backdoor Roth just turned into an unexpected tax bill.

The pro-rata calculation uses your December 31 IRA balance for the year of conversion. So even if you contribute in January and convert in February, the year-end balance determines the taxable percentage.

How to solve the pro-rata problem: The most common solution is to roll your pre-tax Traditional/SEP/SIMPLE IRA balances into your current employer's 401(k) or a solo 401(k) (if you're self-employed). Once that pre-tax IRA money moves into a 401(k), it's no longer counted in the pro-rata calculation. Your IRA slate is clean, and your next backdoor contribution converts tax-free.

Not all 401(k) plans accept incoming rollovers, so check with your plan administrator first. But many do — and for high earners doing backdoor Roths, this is often the necessary setup step.

Mega Backdoor Roth: The Supercharged Version

If you want to go truly big, and your 401(k) plan allows it, there's an even more powerful strategy: the Mega Backdoor Roth.

In 2026, the total contribution limit to a 401(k) (employee + employer contributions combined) is $72,000. The employee elective deferral limit is $24,500. That leaves a potential gap of up to $47,500 that can be filled with after-tax contributions — separate from your regular pre-tax or Roth contributions.

If your plan allows:

  1. Make after-tax contributions up to the $72,000 total limit
  2. Immediately convert those after-tax contributions to Roth (in-plan Roth conversion) or roll them to a Roth IRA

This allows high earners to effectively put $47,500+ per year into a Roth — far beyond the standard $7,500 IRA limit. Over decades, this strategy can supercharge a tax-free retirement nest egg.

The catch: most 401(k) plans don't allow after-tax contributions and in-service withdrawals. You need to specifically check if your plan supports this. Tech companies, some large corporations, and certain professional service firms often have plans that do. If yours doesn't, advocate for it during benefits season.

A Few More Rules to Know

The five-year rule: Roth conversions each start their own five-year clock. If you withdraw converted funds before five years have passed AND before age 59½, you'll owe a 10% penalty (though not ordinary income taxes). This only applies to converted funds — your original direct contributions to a Roth IRA can be withdrawn any time penalty-free.

Age matters: If you're already over 59½, the five-year rule for conversions doesn't trigger the penalty on withdrawals. The Roth IRA itself still needs to have been open for five years for earnings to be tax-free.

Roth IRA vs. Roth 401(k): If your employer offers a Roth 401(k) option, you can contribute up to $24,500 (2026 limit) directly in Roth form with no income limits. This is separate from and in addition to a Roth IRA. Many high earners use both — Roth 401(k) through work and backdoor Roth IRA through their brokerage.

Conversions are irreversible. The old strategy of "recharacterizing" a conversion (undoing it) was eliminated by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017. Once you convert, you've converted. Plan accordingly.

The Bottom Line

The Backdoor Roth IRA is one of the most powerful and underutilized tools in the high-earner's financial toolkit. If you're above the Roth IRA income limits, doing nothing means leaving decades of tax-free growth on the table.

The process is straightforward: contribute to a Traditional IRA (non-deductible), convert promptly to Roth before any earnings accumulate, file Form 8606 every single year, and clear out pre-tax IRA balances first if the pro-rata rule applies.

Yes, it requires a bit more paperwork than just clicking "contribute to Roth." But the math — decades of tax-free compounding on an extra $7,500+ annually — makes it worth every form. The back door is open. Walk through it.
