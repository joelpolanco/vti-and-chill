Your 401(k) Is Probably Ripping You Off (Here's How to Fix It)

Hidden fees drain your retirement savings while you're busy living your life. Here is where to find them and what to do about it.

The 30-Second Version

If you only have 30 seconds, here is the whole argument. The rest of the post is the receipts.

  • Your 401(k) has invisible fees deducted automatically from your balance — most participants have no idea how much they're paying or that the fees exist at all.
  • A 1% fee difference costs $165,000 on a $100,000 portfolio over 30 years — not because of one bad year, but because of compounding erosion that accelerates as your balance grows.
  • Target date funds vary wildly in cost — the same "2055 fund" strategy might cost 0.08% at one provider and 0.75% at another. Always check the expense ratio of the specific fund in your plan.
  • If your plan is expensive, take the match then go elsewhere — a Roth IRA at Fidelity or Schwab gives you access to VTI at 0.03%, a fraction of what most 401(k) menus charge.
  • Roll old 401(k)s immediately when you change jobs — there is no good reason to leave money sitting in an ex-employer's expensive plan.

Your employer told you about the 401(k) during onboarding, between the health insurance slides and the section about parking decals. You enrolled, maybe got some match, picked some funds that sounded reasonable, and moved on with your life.

That was the moment they got you.

Not your employer, necessarily. Not even the 401(k) itself — which is genuinely a powerful wealth-building tool. What got you was the ecosystem of fees, lackluster fund options, and fine-print charges that quietly drain your retirement savings while you're busy, you know, living your life.

The bad news: your 401(k) might be leaking money every year in fees you didn't know existed. The good news: once you know where to look, you can often fix it — or at least minimize the damage.

The Three Layers of 401(k) Fees

Most 401(k) participants think they pay zero in fees because they never write a check. That's by design. 401(k) fees are deducted automatically from your account balance, making them invisible to casual observers. Let's pull back the curtain.

Layer 1: Fund Expense Ratios

Every mutual fund or ETF in your 401(k) charges an annual expense ratio — a percentage of your balance taken annually to cover fund management costs. These range from as low as 0.03% for a Vanguard index fund to well over 1.5% for actively managed funds.

The difference sounds small. It isn't.

A $100,000 portfolio growing at 7% per year:

  • At 0.10% expense ratio: grows to ~$739,000 over 30 years
  • At 1.00% expense ratio: grows to ~$574,000 over 30 years
  • At 1.50% expense ratio: grows to ~$500,000 over 30 years

That 1% fee difference costs you $165,000 over 30 years. According to the Institute of Business & Finance, the gap between 0.10% and 1.00% is roughly $15,800 at year 10, $59,100 at year 20, and $165,800 at year 30 — because the compounding erosion accelerates as the portfolio grows.

Layer 2: Plan Administrative Fees

Beyond the fund-level costs, your 401(k) plan charges administrative fees to cover the costs of running the plan — recordkeeping, compliance, trustee services, plan management. These are sometimes charged as a flat dollar amount, but more often as a percentage of plan assets.

The 25th edition of the 401(k) Averages Book (released in 2025) found that a $5 million plan carries average total costs of 1.08%, while a $50 million plan averages 0.76%. For smaller plans — companies with $1 million in plan assets and 100 participants — Kiplinger reports total plan costs ranging from 0.87% to 3.56%. Those high-end numbers are genuinely alarming.

Layer 3: Hidden Service Fees

Some plans charge additional fees for specific services: annual account maintenance fees, loan processing fees, investment advice fees, distribution fees when you leave the company. These are disclosed in your plan's fee disclosure document (typically the SAI or Annual Fee Disclosure Notice), but most participants never read them.

The number you need to know: $165,000

A 1% fee difference on a $100,000 starting balance costs you $165,000 over 30 years — not in one catastrophic event, but in slow, invisible compounding erosion that accelerates in your final decade of saving, when your balance is largest. This is why fund selection inside a 401(k) is not a minor housekeeping task. It is one of the highest-leverage financial decisions you will make.

The Target Date Fund Problem

Most 401(k) plans default new enrollees into a target date fund — something like "Vanguard Target Retirement 2055 Fund." These are reasonable default options and genuinely better than doing nothing. But two problems arise frequently.

Problem 1: Not all target date funds are created equal. A Vanguard target date fund might charge 0.08%. A similar-sounding fund from a different provider might charge 0.75% or more — nearly ten times the cost — for essentially the same underlying strategy. Always check the expense ratio of your target date fund specifically.

Problem 2: Your plan might offer better options your target date fund is ignoring. Target date funds contain a mix of stocks, bonds, and sometimes other asset classes based on your projected retirement year. If your plan also offers a low-cost total market index fund and a bond index fund, you might construct the same portfolio at a lower expense ratio by selecting funds individually.

The default isn't always wrong — but it's rarely optimized.

How to Find Out What You're Really Paying

Step one: look up your plan's fund options and their expense ratios. This information is legally required to be disclosed to you.

  1. Log into your 401(k) portal
  2. Navigate to the investment options or fund lineup section
  3. Find the expense ratio for each fund you're in (sometimes labeled "net expense ratio" or "annual fund operating expenses")
  4. Compare to low-cost index fund benchmarks: a total US stock market fund should cost well under 0.10%; actively managed funds will be higher

Step two: review your plan's fee disclosure notice (typically sent annually, or available in your account documents). Look for the plan's administrative fee and any per-participant charges.

Step three: visit your HR or benefits portal and look for the Summary Plan Description (SPD) or the Schedule of Fees. This document is required by ERISA to be available to all participants.

What to Do If Your Plan Is Expensive

If your 401(k) options are limited and expensive, you still have good options:

Option 1: Contribute only up to the employer match, then invest elsewhere. The employer match is free money — always take it. But once you've captured the match, additional contributions to a high-fee 401(k) might be better directed into a Roth IRA (or backdoor Roth IRA) where you have full control over fund selection. Fidelity, Vanguard, and Schwab let you hold VTI at 0.03% — a fraction of what most 401(k) plan funds charge.

Option 2: Choose the best available option within your plan. Even bad plans usually have one or two low-cost index funds buried in the menu. An S&P 500 index fund at 0.04% available in your plan is almost always better than an actively managed fund at 1.20%. Pick the lowest-cost, broadest-market option available.

Option 3: Advocate for better funds. ERISA requires plan fiduciaries (your employer) to act in participants' best interests. If your plan is loaded with expensive actively managed funds and lacks low-cost index options, you can formally request that your plan administrator add them. Enough employee pressure has led many companies to upgrade their 401(k) lineups. Some employees have even successfully sued their employers for offering excessively costly plans — this area of litigation has grown significantly over the past decade.

Option 4: Roll old 401(k)s to an IRA. When you leave a job, roll your old 401(k) into an IRA immediately. An old employer's 401(k) typically has no reason to be left sitting there, accumulating fees, with limited investment options. A rollover IRA at Fidelity or Schwab lets you invest in virtually any ETF or fund at the lowest possible costs.

The SECURE 2.0 Upside

Not all 401(k) news is bad. The SECURE 2.0 Act increased contribution limits and added the "super catch-up" provision for ages 60–63. For 2026, you can contribute $24,500 per year ($32,500 with standard catch-up at 50+; $35,750 with the super catch-up for ages 60–63). These increases mean the tax-deferred power of a 401(k) — even a moderately expensive one — is real for high earners who max contributions.

The tax savings on maxing a 401(k) can offset significant fee drag, especially in high income years. A $24,500 contribution in the 32% bracket saves you $7,840 in federal taxes immediately. Even with a 0.5% fee disadvantage over low-cost alternatives, that tax savings often wins the math.

The Bottom Line

Your 401(k) is a powerful tool, but the default settings aren't designed for your benefit — they're designed for plan administrators and fund companies. A few hours of attention can be worth hundreds of thousands of dollars over your investing career.

Here's your action checklist:

  • Check the expense ratios on every fund you hold — aim for under 0.20%
  • Review the plan's administrative fee disclosure
  • Always capture the full employer match — it's free money
  • If your plan is expensive, invest only up to the match, then fund a Roth IRA
  • Roll over old 401(k)s to IRAs when you change jobs
  • Advocate for better fund options through HR

The 401(k) isn't the enemy. Hidden fees are. And now you know where to find them.
