Teaching Your Kids About Investing: Start With a Single Share

The most powerful financial education isn't lectured — it's demonstrated. And it starts with one share and a real account with real stakes.

The 30-Second Version

If you only have 30 seconds, here is the whole argument. The rest of the post is the receipts.

  • Time is the asset: $1,000 invested at birth plus $100/month until 18 grows to ~$48,000 — then compounds to ~$830,000 by age 60 with zero additional contributions.
  • Open the account: A UGMA or UTMA custodial account takes about 15 minutes at Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard. You control it until they reach adulthood.
  • Buy one share they recognize: Ownership changes how kids think. One share of a company they use makes them an owner, not just a consumer.
  • Teach the lesson alongside the account: The money is not the education. The experience of watching it grow, drop, and recover over years is the education.
  • If they have earned income: A custodial Roth IRA at 16 with decades of tax-free compounding ahead is one of the most powerful financial gifts you can give.

Your kid knows how to navigate YouTube without touching the home screen. They can identify every Marvel character by costume detail. They've somehow memorized the entire lyrics to songs you've never heard of. But ask them what a stock is, and you'll get a shrug that suggests the concept might as well be ancient Sumerian.

Here's the thing: the financial education system has completely failed the next generation. Most kids graduate high school without ever learning what compound interest is, what a brokerage account does, or why saving 10% of every paycheck starting at 22 is worth more than saving 25% starting at 35. This isn't an accident — a financially illiterate population is a boon to the financial services industry, which makes its money selling complexity to confused people.

You can change this for your kid. And you don't need to be a CFA or sit them down for a four-hour lecture. The most powerful financial education isn't taught — it's demonstrated. And it starts with one share.

Why Starting Early Is the Actual Point

Let's run the math that should make every parent a little uncomfortable about waiting.

If you open a custodial investment account for your child at birth and invest $1,000 in a total market index fund, then add $100/month until they turn 18, they'll have roughly $48,000 at age 18 assuming a 7% annual return. Not revolutionary on its own.

But leave that $48,000 completely untouched — no more contributions — and at age 60, compounding turns it into approximately $830,000. From $48,000 to $830,000, just by waiting and being invested in boring index funds. Your child's entire retirement funded mostly by the time you gave the money to work.

Now here's what makes this even more interesting as a teaching tool: if that 18-year-old sees their account, watches it grow, understands why it grows, and learns not to panic when it drops — you haven't just given them money. You've given them financial intuition. That's worth more than the account balance.

The earlier kids are introduced to investing — not just told about it, but shown it through a real account with real money — the more naturally the core principles become part of their thinking. Compound growth, patience, riding out volatility: these aren't concepts you learn once in a classroom. They're habits of mind built through years of watching money behave the way money behaves.

The Mechanics: Custodial Accounts and How They Work

A custodial account — most commonly a UGMA (Uniform Gifts to Minors Act) or UTMA (Uniform Transfers to Minors Act) account — lets you invest on behalf of a minor. You control the account as the custodian until the child reaches the age of majority (18 or 21 depending on your state), at which point the account becomes legally theirs.

Virtually every major brokerage offers custodial accounts: Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard. You can open one in about 15 minutes. You can invest in any ETF you'd buy for yourself — including VTI.

A few practical notes:

  • The "kiddie tax" rules mean that unearned income above a certain threshold ($2,500 in 2024) for children under 19 is taxed at the parent's rate. For most families with modest contributions, this isn't a major issue.
  • The money is a gift — it legally belongs to the child. Once transferred, you can't take it back.
  • When the child takes control at the age of majority, they can do whatever they want with it, including spending it. This is why the financial education alongside the account matters as much as the account itself.

If your child has earned income — from a part-time job, lawn mowing, babysitting — they can also contribute to a custodial Roth IRA, up to their earned income amount capped at the annual Roth IRA limit. A Roth IRA opened at 16 with a few thousand dollars has extraordinary potential due to the decades of tax-free compounding ahead of it.

The real gift isn't the money — it's the intuition

Financial intuition isn't something you can lecture into a kid. It develops from years of lived experience: watching a balance grow slowly, seeing it drop during a market correction, and then watching it recover and continue higher. A 10-year-old who sees their account fall 20% during a bear market and then hits an all-time high two years later has learned something no classroom can teach. They've experienced that volatility is noise. That lesson will be worth tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars over their lifetime — not because they stayed calm during that particular drop, but because they'll stay calm during every drop that follows.

Age-Appropriate Money Lessons That Actually Stick

The account is the mechanism. The education is the point.

Ages 5–8: The basics of delayed gratification. This is where it starts — not with index funds, but with the concept that waiting pays off. The classic marshmallow experiment, the savings jar, the "save half of your birthday money" rule. At this age, the lesson is: money isn't for spending the moment you get it.

A tool that works well here: the three-jar system. Label three jars "Spend," "Save," and "Give." When your kid gets allowance or birthday money, they split it. The Save jar connects to their real account later.

Ages 9–12: Ownership and business basics. This is the age to introduce ownership. Buy your child one or two shares of a company they recognize — Disney, Apple, whatever they're actually interested in. Not because these are great portfolio picks (they're not — stick to index funds for serious money), but because ownership changes how they think. They'll start paying attention to news about that company. They'll notice the price go up and down. They're not just a consumer anymore — they're an owner.

Use this as a jumping-off point to explain what a stock actually is: a tiny piece of a real company. When you buy VTI, you own tiny pieces of Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, and about 3,500 other American businesses. Every time people buy iPhones or search Google or order from Amazon, those companies make money, and a little bit of that flows back to you.

Ages 13–17: Compounding and savings rates. Pull up a compound interest calculator together. Ask them: if you put $1,000 in today and never touched it, how much would it be worth when you're 65? Let them run the numbers. The result will surprise them.

This is also the age to introduce the idea of the savings rate — that how much you keep matters more than how much you earn. A teenager who internalizes "I should save at least 20% of every dollar I earn" before they enter the workforce is set up better than any portfolio selection could accomplish.

Ages 18+: The full picture. Roth IRA, 401(k) contributions, the power of employer match, expense ratios, the difference between an index fund and an actively managed fund. Most young adults receive zero of this education before their first real job. Be the parent who changes that.

The Bottom Line

You don't need to raise a finance prodigy. You need to raise a kid who understands three things: money grows when you invest it, time is your biggest asset, and simple beats complicated. Start with a custodial account and a single share. Show them the account growing. Let them watch a market drop and recover. Make compound interest personal.

The best financial education isn't a lecture — it's a real account with real stakes, a patient parent who explains what's happening, and the lived experience of watching boring index funds do their boring, magnificent work over time.

Open the account this weekend. The best time was ten years ago. The second best time is now.
