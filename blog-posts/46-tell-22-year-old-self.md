# What I'd Tell My 22-Year-Old Self About Money

Dear 22-year-old you,

I know what you're thinking about right now. It's probably not your Roth IRA contribution limit. You're thinking about the weekend, about your job that maybe you kind of like, about whether you have enough in checking to cover rent and also go out Saturday. Retirement is a concept that applies to other people — people who wear khakis to work and have opinions about lawn mowers. You're just getting started.

Which is exactly why this letter matters more than any financial advice you'll get for the next 20 years.

---

## The Single Most Powerful Thing You Can Do Right Now

You have something that no amount of money, intelligence, or ambition can ever buy back: time.

Here's what that means in practice. If you invest $300 per month starting at 22 and stop completely at 32 — just 10 years of contributions — and then leave that money untouched until 65, you'll have more money than someone who invests the same $300/month from age 32 to 65 without ever stopping. Thirty-three years of contributions versus ten. You win, just by starting first. That's not motivational poster math. That's actual arithmetic powered by compound interest.

At 22, every year you wait costs you roughly two years of future wealth. The money you put in at 22 has 43 years to compound before traditional retirement age. The money you put in at 30 has 35 years. That 8-year difference, at 7% annual returns, means each dollar invested at 22 is worth about $21 at 65. The same dollar invested at 30 is worth $11.60 at 65. Same dollar. Nearly double the result.

This is the thing nobody tells you at 22, and it's the only thing that actually matters: start. Now. Whatever amount you can. The number is almost irrelevant compared to the timing.

If you can only put $50/month right now, put $50. If you get a raise, bump it. Automate it so you never see the money hit your checking account. Future you will be embarrassingly grateful.

## The Mistakes That Feel Like Smart Moves

A few things that seem smart but will quietly cost you a fortune:

**Trying to pick winning stocks.** I know. The stock-picking thing sounds exciting, and maybe you'll catch a run on something and feel like a genius for a few months. Enjoy it. Then remember that over 15-year periods, roughly 90% of actively managed funds fail to beat the S&P 500. If professional fund managers with Bloomberg terminals and research teams can't beat the index consistently, the probability that you can is essentially statistical noise. Buy VTI. Hold it forever. That's the whole strategy.

**Cashing out your 401(k) when you change jobs.** You'll be tempted to do this. The balance will seem small, maybe $8,000 or $12,000, and there will be something you want to buy or a bill that would go away. Don't do it. First, you'll pay income tax plus a 10% early withdrawal penalty, which means you're handing $2,000-3,000 to the IRS immediately. Second, and more importantly, that $12,000 at 22, left alone and invested, is worth nearly $250,000 at 65 at a 7% return. You'd be selling a quarter million dollars for $9,000. Roll it over to an IRA instead. Takes one phone call.

**Waiting until you "have your act together" to start.** This is the most expensive procrastination habit in personal finance. There's always a reason to wait — student loans to pay off, the move to a new city, the job change, the relationship, the thing. The optimal time to start investing was the day you got your first paycheck. The second optimal time is today, whatever day you're reading this. You don't need a plan. You don't need to understand every nuance. You need an account, an automated contribution, and VTI.

**Ignoring your employer's 401(k) match.** If your employer matches your 401(k) contributions up to 3% or 4% of your salary, not maxing out to get that full match is the equivalent of refusing a portion of your paycheck. The match is a 50%-100% instant return on your investment before a single day of market return. No other investment on Earth offers this. Max the match. Immediately. Non-negotiable.

## The Money Truths Nobody Teaches You

A few things you'll figure out eventually, but faster is better:

**Your savings rate matters more than your income.** A person earning $60,000 and saving 30% is building more wealth than a person earning $100,000 and saving 5%. The number on your paycheck is less important than the gap between what you earn and what you spend. Widen that gap.

**Financial anxiety doesn't go away when you earn more — it transforms.** At 22, you worry about making rent. At 35, you worry about whether you saved enough. At 55, you worry about whether you saved enough to actually retire. The anxiety scales with the stakes unless you build actual systems. The system is: automate savings, invest in boring index funds, check your accounts quarterly (not daily), and trust the math. The anxiety quiets when the system is in place, not when the balance hits a specific number.

**Debt is not normal, and it compounds against you.** The financial services industry has worked very hard to normalize debt — car loans, student loans, credit card revolving balances, buy-now-pay-later everything. Credit card debt at 24% APR is the inverse of compound interest. It's compound destruction. Pay it off aggressively. Stay out of high-interest debt the way you'd stay out of a casino — you might win occasionally, but the house always wins long-term.

**The stock market will terrify you multiple times.** There will be crashes. Real ones — 30%, 40%, 50% drops that feel permanent and are accompanied by media coverage that strongly implies civilization might be ending. Every single time in history, markets have recovered and gone on to new highs. Every time. Your job during crashes is to do nothing. Or, if you're brave and have cash available, buy more. The people who got rich in the stock market weren't smarter than everyone else. They were calmer.

**Compound interest works in reverse too.** The sooner you understand this, the better. Carrying a $5,000 credit card balance at 24% APR doesn't just cost you the interest on $5,000. It costs you the investment returns you would have earned if that $5,000 was invested instead of borrowed. The real cost of debt is the opportunity cost — the wealth you're not building because you're paying interest instead.

One last thing, 22-year-old self: don't let perfect be the enemy of started. You don't need to understand every investment concept. You don't need the perfect portfolio. You need a brokerage account, automated monthly investments in VTI, and the discipline to keep contributing when the market drops and everyone around you is panicking.

That's it. The formula is embarrassingly simple. The hard part is believing it's enough.

It is.
