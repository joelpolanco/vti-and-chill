# The Sunk Cost Fallacy: When to Cut Losses (And When to Hold)

You bought shares of a company at $50. It's now at $22. You've lost 56% of your investment. The original reasons you bought it — a new product launch, an expected contract, the CEO's optimistic guidance — have all failed to materialize. The sector is struggling. Competitors are winning. The thesis is dead.

Do you sell?

Your rational financial advisor would say: evaluate the company on its current merits, compare it to alternative uses of that $22 per share, and make a forward-looking decision that ignores the sunk cost entirely.

Your brain says: *I can't sell at a loss. I need to get back to even first. What if it recovers? I'll just hold a bit longer.*

This is the sunk cost fallacy, and it is responsible for some of the most spectacular investor losses in financial history — not because people held through temporary volatility, but because they held through permanent decline, refusing to acknowledge that the money was gone and that continuing to hold was making it worse.

But here's the part that rarely gets enough attention: the sunk cost fallacy isn't just about when to sell. It's also about understanding the fundamental difference between holding a declining individual stock (where the fallacy is usually dangerous) and holding a declining index fund (where what looks like sunk cost thinking is actually just correct long-term investing). Getting this distinction right is worth real money.

---

## What the Research Says About Sunk Costs

The sunk cost fallacy was rigorously documented by Kahneman and Tversky as part of their broader work on prospect theory, and subsequently explored extensively by Richard Thaler — the father of behavioral economics and Nobel laureate in Economics 2017.

Thaler's research demonstrated that the psychological relationship between people and their previous financial commitments is deeply irrational. In a now-famous experiment, subjects were asked to choose between a $50 ski trip ticket they'd already paid for versus a free ticket to a better ski resort on the same day. The majority chose to go to the resort they'd already paid for — even though the free resort was better — because it felt wasteful to "waste" the money they'd already spent.

But the money was already gone regardless of which resort they chose. The sunk cost — what they'd already paid — was irrelevant to the forward-looking decision of where to have the best day skiing. It was already spent.

A 2018 study published in *Psychology Research and Behavior Management* found that cognitive dissonance plays a central role in the sunk cost effect: the more invested you are in a decision (financially, emotionally, reputationally), the harder it is to walk away, because doing so forces you to confront that you made a mistake. Holding on keeps the mistake hypothetical. Selling makes it real.

For investors, this means the very act of buying a stock creates psychological commitment that biases you toward holding it longer than the evidence supports.

## Individual Stocks and the Sunk Cost Trap

The individual stock version of the sunk cost fallacy is genuinely destructive because individual companies can go to zero. The S&P 500 cannot.

Enron. WorldCom. Lehman Brothers. Bed Bath & Beyond. Each of these companies attracted long-term investors — people who had held for years, seen the positions appreciate, and then watched them decline. At every stage of the decline, sunk cost thinking provided psychological ammunition for continued holding:

- "I'm already down 30%, I can't sell now."
- "I've held this for five years. I'm not selling after one bad quarter."
- "The CEO says the fundamentals are strong. I believe in the company."
- "I need to get back to even first."

Enron went from $90 to zero. Lehman Brothers went from $85 to zero. Bed Bath & Beyond went bankrupt in 2023, making its shares worthless. Every investor who held "until it came back" held all the way to zero.

There is no coming back from zero. That's what makes individual stock investing fundamentally different from index fund investing — and it's why the sunk cost calculus is completely different depending on what you're holding.

The economic definition of a sunk cost is money already spent that cannot be recovered and should not affect future decisions. Once you've recognized that the original investment thesis for an individual company is no longer valid — the competitive position has eroded, the balance sheet is deteriorating, the management has lost credibility — the rational action is to evaluate the company on its current merits and current prospects, independent of what you paid for it.

This is genuinely hard. It requires admitting you were wrong. It requires explicitly rejecting the narrative your brain is constructing about recovery and eventual vindication. It requires selling at $22 when you bought at $50, knowing full well that selling locks in a loss.

But the alternative — holding all the way to zero — is worse.

## The Index Fund Exception: When Holding Through Losses Is Correct

Here is where the financial education is critical, because the sunk cost framework that applies to individual stocks should not be applied to broad market index funds.

When VTI falls 34% in March 2020, the correct action is to hold — and if possible, to buy more. This is not sunk cost thinking. This is accurate long-term analysis.

The difference is structural:
- An individual company can go bankrupt, become permanently impaired, or lose its competitive position permanently. The original investment thesis can be wrong in ways that never recover.
- A total market index fund cannot go to zero as long as the US economy exists. It holds thousands of companies. When individual companies fail, they're replaced. The index is self-cleansing. The long-term trajectory reflects the underlying growth of the American economy.

When you hold VTI through a 34% decline, you are not engaging in sunk cost thinking. You are correctly identifying that this is a temporary disruption to a long-term upward trend, and that selling would lock in temporary losses while missing the inevitable recovery.

The practical test is this: ask yourself whether the fundamental underlying reason for your investment has changed.

For VTI: has the long-term productivity growth of the US economy permanently ended? Has capitalism stopped working? Are corporate profits expected to go to zero forever? No. The decline is temporary volatility. Hold.

For a speculative individual stock: has the competitive advantage disappeared? Has the revenue growth story collapsed? Is the balance sheet unsustainable? If yes, the original thesis is broken. The sunk cost fallacy says hold. Rational analysis says sell.

## The Reddit Stories That Illustrate the Difference

A r/investing post from 2022 described a user who had held Peloton from its COVID-era highs near $170 all the way down to $8 — a 95% decline. At every stage, they held because they couldn't stand to realize the loss. "I'll sell when it gets back to $50" became "I'll sell when it gets back to $30" became "I'll sell when it gets back to $20." By the time they posted, they were down 95% and asking the community whether they should sell.

The painful irony: if this investor had taken the same dollar amount and put it in VTI at any point during that decline, they would have significantly recovered by 2023.

Contrast this with the countless posts from investors who sold their VTI or broad index fund holdings during the COVID crash in March 2020 — also to avoid the pain of continuing to hold through losses. These investors made the opposite error: they treated their index fund as though it had the same risk profile as an individual stock, and "cut their losses" on an investment that was going to recover.

Both groups were influenced by sunk cost thinking. One group used it to hold too long. The other used a version of it — the fear of accumulated losses — to sell too soon. The solution in both cases is the same: evaluate the underlying investment, not the psychology of your entry price.

## A Framework for Cutting Losses Without the Fallacy

When evaluating whether to sell a declining position, the question to ask is not "how much am I down?" The question is: "If I had this amount of money in cash today, would I buy this investment at its current price?"

If the answer is yes — if the investment still makes sense at its current price given current information — hold.
If the answer is no — the thesis is broken, the fundamentals have deteriorated, there are better uses for this capital — sell.

The entry price is irrelevant to this calculation. Pretend you don't know what you paid for it. Evaluate only what the investment is worth today and what it's likely to be worth in the future.

For a total market index fund, this calculation almost always points toward holding through downturns, because the underlying thesis (the US economy will grow over time) remains intact regardless of short-term volatility.

For individual speculative investments where the thesis has genuinely broken down, this calculation often points toward selling — even at a loss — and redeploying the remaining capital into something with better prospects.

---

## The Bottom Line

The sunk cost fallacy tells investors that past losses justify continued holding, regardless of future prospects. This is wrong in every context: what you paid for something in the past is irrelevant to what you should do with it today.

For individual stocks where the investment thesis has failed, the sunk cost fallacy can trap you in a declining position all the way to zero. The rational move is to evaluate the forward prospects honestly, ignore the entry price, and sell if the thesis is broken.

For a total market index fund like VTI, what looks like sunk cost thinking — holding through 34% declines — is actually correct long-term analysis, because the underlying thesis (the US economy will grow) hasn't changed. The mistake there is selling, not holding.

Knowing which situation you're in is the entire game. And the simplest way to avoid playing the wrong game is to hold index funds instead of individual stocks — so you never have to make the individual stock sunk cost calculation in the first place.
