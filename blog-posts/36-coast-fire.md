# The Coast FIRE Calculator: How Much Do You Really Need to Save?

Most retirement conversations go like this: "Save as much as you can, for as long as you can, and retire when you have enough." It's not bad advice, but it's a bit like saying "drive as fast as you safely can until you get there." Technically correct, practically exhausting.

What if there was a point — a specific, calculable dollar amount — at which you could stop saving for retirement entirely and just let compounding carry you the rest of the way?

That's Coast FIRE. And the number might be smaller than you think.

## What Coast FIRE Actually Means

Coast FIRE is the point where your existing retirement savings, left alone to grow at a historical market rate, will compound to your target retirement number by your target retirement age — without you ever adding another dollar.

Once you hit your Coast FIRE number, you've done your retirement saving job. You don't need to save anymore (for retirement — you might still save for other goals). You can take a lower-paying job you love, cut your hours, pivot to a passion project, or simply stop stressing about maxing every account. Your future self's retirement is already on autopilot.

The metaphor is a bicycle. You've pedaled hard up a steep hill. Once you reach the top, you can stop pedaling and coast all the way down. The momentum does the work.

The critical insight: **you don't need to save all the money you'll retire on.** You only need to save enough that compound growth finishes the job.

## The Formula (It's Simpler Than It Looks)

Coast FIRE uses basic time-value-of-money math.

**Step 1: Calculate your FIRE number (your retirement target)**

FIRE Number = Annual Retirement Expenses × 25

This is based on the 4% safe withdrawal rate from the Trinity Study, which found that withdrawing 4% annually from a diversified portfolio has historically lasted 30+ years in nearly all scenarios.

If you plan to spend $60,000 per year in retirement, your FIRE number is $1,500,000.

**Step 2: Calculate your Coast FIRE number**

Coast FIRE Number = FIRE Number ÷ (1 + r)^n

Where:
- **r** = expected annual return (typically 6–7% real returns after inflation)
- **n** = number of years until your target retirement age

**A worked example:**

- Target annual retirement spending: $60,000
- FIRE number: $60,000 × 25 = **$1,500,000**
- Current age: 35, target retirement age: 65 → n = 30 years
- Expected annual return: 7%

Coast FIRE Number = $1,500,000 ÷ (1.07)^30 = $1,500,000 ÷ 7.61 = **$197,105**

If you have $197,105 invested today at 35, and you never add another penny, that money will grow to $1,500,000 by age 65 at a 7% average annual return.

That's your coasting point. Everything you contribute after that just accelerates the timeline.

**Adjusting for different variables:**

| Annual spending | FIRE number | Coast at 35 (30 yrs, 7%) | Coast at 40 (25 yrs, 7%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $40,000 | $1,000,000 | $131,367 | $184,249 |
| $60,000 | $1,500,000 | $197,105 | $276,374 |
| $80,000 | $2,000,000 | $262,807 | $368,499 |
| $100,000 | $2,500,000 | $328,509 | $460,624 |

The numbers drop dramatically when you're younger. At age 25 with 40 years to retirement (still at 7%), a $1,500,000 FIRE number requires only about $100,000 in current savings to Coast. Start early and the compounding engine does heavy lifting.

## What You Still Need After Reaching Coast

Hitting your Coast FIRE number doesn't mean you're done working or that money stops mattering. It means you're off the hook for *retirement* savings. You still need to cover current living expenses.

This is where "Barista FIRE" and "Coast FIRE" intersect in popular discussion: you've saved enough for retirement, so now you can take a lower-stress, lower-income job — something that covers your bills without requiring max retirement contributions. The pressure is off.

What you still need to fund from ongoing income:
- Current monthly expenses (housing, food, transportation, utilities, insurance)
- Health insurance (if your new, lower-stress job doesn't provide it — this is a significant consideration)
- Any non-retirement savings goals (kids' college, home purchase, etc.)

What you no longer *need* to do:
- Contribute to retirement accounts (though you certainly can)
- Maximize every tax-advantaged space
- Take high-earning/high-stress jobs purely for income

Some Coast FIRE adherents use this milestone as permission to take a sabbatical, change careers, move abroad cheaply, or pursue creative or entrepreneurial work that wouldn't otherwise pay enough. The freedom isn't financial independence in the traditional sense — you still need income for daily life. But the retirement anxiety dissolves.

## The Risks and Reality Checks

Coast FIRE math is powerful but makes assumptions that deserve scrutiny.

**Return assumption risk:** A 7% real return is reasonable based on long-term US stock market history, but is not guaranteed. If you assume 7% and average 5%, your $197,105 only grows to about $826,000 by 65 — well short of your $1,500,000 target. Conservative planners use 5–6% to build in margin.

**Inflation:** The example above uses a 7% real return, meaning after inflation. If you're using nominal returns (say, 10% historical average for the S&P 500), you need to also grow your spending estimate with inflation to get an apples-to-apples comparison. Most Coast FIRE calculators handle this automatically; just be clear about whether the rate is real or nominal.

**Sequence of returns at retirement:** Even if your Coast number works out on average, how the market performs in your early retirement years matters enormously. This is covered in depth in our post on sequence of returns risk — but the short version is: the average return matters less than the *order* of returns when you're withdrawing.

**Life changes:** You might not retire at 65. You might need more money for healthcare. Your lifestyle spending might increase. Coast FIRE gives you a framework, not a guarantee.

The solution to most of these risks: **be conservative with your assumptions** and build a margin of safety. If your Coast number comes out to $200,000, you might feel better not truly coasting until you hit $250,000. No rule says you have to stop contributing at the exact Coast number.

## How to Calculate Your Own Coast FIRE Number

Here's the process:

1. **Estimate your annual retirement spending.** Be realistic. Many people use their current spending minus mortgage (paid off) and work costs (commuting, professional clothing). Don't forget healthcare.

2. **Multiply by 25** to get your FIRE number.

3. **Decide your target retirement age** — your current age subtracted from this is **n**.

4. **Choose your return assumption** — 6% is conservative, 7% is moderate. Use the formula:

   Coast FIRE = FIRE Number ÷ (1 + r)^n

5. **Compare to your current retirement savings.** If you're above your Coast number — congratulations. If not — you know exactly how much you need to save before you can ease up.

Free Coast FIRE calculators are widely available online. Plug in your numbers on multiple sites and see where you land. The math is simple enough to do in a spreadsheet.

## The Bottom Line

Coast FIRE is one of the most liberating concepts in personal finance. It transforms "save forever" into a concrete, achievable milestone. Once you know your number, every dollar you invest either moves you toward it or proves you've already crossed it.

You don't need $2 million in the bank by next Tuesday. You just need enough for compounding to carry you the rest of the way. And depending on your age and expenses, that might be a lot closer than you think.

Pedal hard early. Then coast.
