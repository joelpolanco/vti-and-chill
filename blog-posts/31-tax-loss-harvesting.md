# Tax-Loss Harvesting: How to Turn Market Drops Into Tax Savings

The market just dropped 10%. Your portfolio is down. You're staring at red numbers wondering what went wrong with your chill investing strategy.

Here's the thing: that dip you're dreading? It might be a gift. Not a fun gift, like a bonus. More like a gift you didn't ask for but turns out to be surprisingly useful — like a rebate check arriving on a bad day.

That gift is called tax-loss harvesting, and it's one of the few legitimate ways individual investors can squeeze extra value out of a down market. You don't need a Wall Street pedigree or a Bloomberg terminal to use it. You just need to understand a few rules and know when to act.

Let's break it down.

## What Is Tax-Loss Harvesting and Why Does It Matter?

Tax-loss harvesting is the practice of selling an investment that has declined in value in order to realize a capital loss — and then using that loss to offset capital gains or ordinary income on your tax return.

The IRS taxes your investment profits. But it also lets you subtract your investment losses. When you realize a loss, that loss can cancel out gains you've made elsewhere, reducing the taxes you owe. Any losses that exceed your gains can be used to offset up to $3,000 of ordinary income per year. And here's the kicker — if your losses are larger than that, you can carry them forward into future tax years indefinitely.

Here's a simple example. Say you own two funds. Fund A has a $5,000 gain for the year. Fund B — which took a hit when the market sold off — has an unrealized loss of $5,000. If you sell Fund B to realize that $5,000 loss, it directly cancels out Fund A's $5,000 gain. Net capital gains tax owed: zero.

You've essentially taken a market dip and turned it into a tax break. Then you reinvest the proceeds into a similar (but not identical) fund and continue riding the market.

A few important notes on how the IRS classifies gains and losses:

- **Short-term capital gains** (assets held less than a year) are taxed at your ordinary income tax rate — which can be as high as 37%.
- **Long-term capital gains** (assets held more than a year) are taxed at the lower capital gains rates of 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income. For 2026, the 0% rate applies to single filers with taxable income up to $49,450, and married filers up to $98,900.

When you harvest losses, short-term losses first offset short-term gains, and long-term losses offset long-term gains. If there's any excess, it can cross categories. This matters because sheltering short-term gains (taxed at your full income rate) with harvested losses is often the most valuable outcome.

## The Wash-Sale Rule: The Trap That Catches Beginners

Here's where people mess up. The IRS isn't naive. If you could sell a fund at a loss and immediately buy it back, everyone would do it constantly and the tax code would be a joke. So the IRS created the **wash-sale rule**.

The wash-sale rule says: if you sell a security at a loss and buy a "substantially identical" security within 30 calendar days before or after the sale, your loss is **disallowed**. Poof. Gone. The IRS adds the disallowed loss to the cost basis of the replacement security, so you're not permanently losing it — but you lose the immediate tax benefit entirely.

What makes something "substantially identical"? The IRS doesn't define it with surgical precision, but the practical rule is:
- You cannot sell VTI and immediately buy VTI back.
- You cannot sell SPY (an S&P 500 ETF) and buy IVV (another S&P 500 ETF) — those track the same index and are considered substantially identical.
- You **can** sell VTI (total US market) and buy ITOT (iShares total US market) — some argue these are different enough, but it's a gray area.
- You **can** sell VTI and buy VXUS (international) — completely different asset class.
- You **can** sell an S&P 500 fund and buy a total market fund like VTI — different index, different exposure.

The 30-day window runs in both directions: 30 days before the sale AND 30 days after. [According to Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/personal-finance/wash-sales-rules-tax), the wash-sale rule also applies across different accounts you own or control — including your IRA and your spouse's accounts. So don't think you can sell at a loss in your taxable account and buy back in your Roth IRA. The IRS sees through that.

The cleanest approach is to swap into a similar-but-not-identical fund and wait at least 31 days before switching back if you want your original holding. Plenty of investors swap VTI for Schwab's SCHB, or an S&P 500 fund for a total market fund, to stay invested in the same asset class while keeping the tax loss clean.

## When Does Tax-Loss Harvesting Actually Make Sense?

Tax-loss harvesting sounds compelling, but it's not magic — and it's not always worth doing.

**It makes the most sense when:**

1. **You're in a high tax bracket.** If you're in the 22% bracket or higher, reducing taxable income by harvesting losses has real dollar value. If you're in the 10% or 12% bracket, you might already owe 0% on long-term capital gains, making the benefit minimal.

2. **You have significant unrealized losses.** Markets correct regularly. The 2020 COVID crash, the 2022 bear market — these create harvesting opportunities. You only benefit from losses you actually realize.

3. **You have gains to offset.** Harvesting losses into the void (no gains to offset) still gives you the $3,000 annual ordinary income deduction and carryforward, which has value — but is less immediately powerful.

4. **Your taxable brokerage account is large enough to matter.** Tax-loss harvesting only works in taxable accounts. You can't harvest losses inside a Roth IRA, traditional IRA, or 401(k) — those accounts have their own tax treatment.

**Where it gets more complicated:**

Tax-loss harvesting doesn't eliminate taxes — it **defers** them. When you sell Fund B at a loss and buy a substitute, your cost basis in the new fund is lower. Eventually, when you sell the substitute, you'll owe taxes on a larger gain than you would have. You've essentially borrowed a tax deduction from your future self.

However, if you hold the replacement fund until death, your heirs may receive a stepped-up cost basis, effectively making the deferred gain disappear. That's a powerful long-term strategy. And even if you do eventually pay the tax, you've had use of those deferred dollars — which themselves compounded in the meantime.

Some sophisticated investors use robo-advisors like Betterment or Wealthfront that automate tax-loss harvesting daily. Research by Wealthfront has suggested automated daily harvesting can add 0.5–1.5% annually in after-tax returns for accounts with regular contributions, though results vary significantly by tax situation and market conditions.

**The bottom line on timing:** Tax-loss harvesting works best during market dips, not after recoveries. You need an unrealized loss to harvest. Once a position has bounced back, the window is closed. Keep an eye on your portfolio during significant drawdowns — that's when opportunity knocks.

## The Bottom Line

Tax-loss harvesting is one of the few financial strategies that literally pays you to pay attention during a market downturn. Instead of panic-selling or paralysis, you can make a thoughtful, tax-smart move that improves your after-tax returns without changing your long-term investment strategy.

The core mechanics:
- Sell losing positions to realize capital losses
- Use those losses to offset capital gains (or up to $3,000 of ordinary income per year)
- Immediately reinvest in a similar-but-not-identical fund to stay invested
- Respect the 30-day wash-sale window or your loss is disallowed
- Carry unused losses forward to future tax years

Is it worth doing automatically? Possibly, if your brokerage or robo-advisor supports it. Is it worth manually hunting every quarter? Probably not unless you have large taxable accounts and meaningful losses available. But the next time markets drop and your portfolio flashes red, remember: you're not just watching losses. You might be watching an opportunity.

The silver lining was always in the tax code. You just have to know where to look.
