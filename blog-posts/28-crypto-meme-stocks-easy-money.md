# Crypto Bros, Meme Stocks, and the Eternal Search for Easy Money

Let me tell you about a market that became completely untethered from fundamental value. Speculators poured in from all walks of life. Prices rose so dramatically that people started quitting their jobs to trade full-time. Stories of extraordinary gains spread through word of mouth and popular media. Sophisticated financial instruments were created to allow even more people to participate. Politicians and mainstream commentators debated whether this was a genuine revolution in asset pricing or a dangerous bubble.

Everyone said it was different this time.

Then it crashed.

I'm describing tulip mania — the Dutch tulip bubble of 1636-1637, widely considered the first documented speculative bubble in financial history. At the peak of the frenzy, a single Semper Augustus tulip bulb sold for the equivalent of a skilled laborer's annual salary multiplied by several times over. Futures contracts on tulip bulbs created an entire speculative derivatives market. Fortunes were made and lost on flowers.

And then, on February 3, 1637, the market collapsed. Prices fell to near zero. The same human psychology that had driven rational Dutch merchants to pay absurd prices for flower bulbs worked in reverse, sending prices crashing as quickly as they had risen.

Nearly 400 years later, we are still running this playbook. The technology changes. The asset changes. The cultural context changes. The human psychology does not.

## The Psychology That Never Changes

Robert Shiller, the Yale economist who won the Nobel Prize in part for his work identifying speculative bubbles, has documented what he calls "narrative economics" — the way compelling stories spread through populations and drive economic behavior. The specific story changes with each cycle, but the structure is consistent.

Every major speculative bubble is driven by a core narrative that makes the asset seem uniquely justified at high valuations. The narrative is always partially true, which is what makes it so compelling. The dotcom bubble was built on a real technological revolution — the internet genuinely transformed civilization. The 2008 housing bubble was built on a real demographic trend — growing household formation and genuine demand for housing. The crypto narrative was built on real technology — blockchain has genuine applications, decentralized finance addresses real problems with traditional banking.

The problem is that real underlying value doesn't justify unlimited valuation. Eventually, the price has to connect to the fundamental reality. When that correction comes, the narrative that sustained the run-up gets replaced instantly by a new narrative that explains why the collapse was inevitable.

The investors who get hurt are not the ones who identified the bubble early and shorted it. They're not the insiders and early adopters who got out at the top. They're the ordinary people who arrived late, bought near the peak, and held through the decline waiting for the original story to reassert itself.

## The Dotcom Crash and Its Descendants

The dot-com bubble of 1999-2000 is the great modern template for speculative excess. The NASDAQ Composite rose 600% between 1995 and its March 2000 peak. Companies with no revenue, no profits, and no coherent path to profitability were valued in the billions. The price-to-sales ratio of companies that went public in 2000 averaged nearly 49 — in an era when a P/S above 3 was considered aggressive.

The narrative was that the internet was going to change everything, and companies that got there first would be unassailable. This was partially true! Amazon and Google did get there first and did become unassailable. But that insight did not justify the valuations of Pets.com, eToys, or 360networks — which all went to zero.

The NASDAQ fell 78% from its March 2000 peak. Investors who had allocated heavily to tech because they didn't want to "miss the internet revolution" waited until April 2015 — fifteen years — to see the index return to its 2000 high.

The GameStop moment of 2021 was a compressed version of the same dynamic. The narrative was emotionally compelling — retail investors versus hedge funds, the little guy fighting back — and it was built on something real (the actual short squeeze mechanics that forced hedge funds to cover). But the narrative didn't justify a company worth $40 billion that was losing money selling physical video games in an increasingly digital world.

People who bought at $300-$400 per share in the excitement of the cultural moment were not investing. They were paying for the feeling of participating in a revolution. The revolution didn't pay dividends.

## The Crypto Cycle: A Case Study in Recurring Mania

Bitcoin was created in 2009. Its first recorded commercial transaction used 10,000 Bitcoin to purchase two pizzas in May 2010 — implying a price of roughly $0.003 per coin. By November 2021, Bitcoin reached $69,000 per coin.

The extraordinary appreciation over twelve years created a population of Bitcoin millionaires and billionaires, and their success stories spread with the virality that only the internet enables. Unlike earlier asset bubbles, which required physical proximity to spread narratives (you had to know someone who was trading tulip futures), crypto mania could travel globally in minutes through Reddit, Twitter, YouTube, and TikTok.

The 2020-2021 crypto surge was driven partly by real factors: low interest rates, unprecedented monetary stimulus, growing institutional adoption, and genuine use cases for blockchain technology. But it was also driven by the same speculative frenzy that drives every bubble. At the peak, a dog-themed meme cryptocurrency (Dogecoin) had a market cap of $80 billion — despite offering no functionality beyond novelty and being explicitly created as a joke.

When the Federal Reserve began raising interest rates in early 2022, speculative assets declined. Bitcoin fell from $69,000 to below $17,000 — a 75% decline. Most altcoins fell further. Many fell 90-99%. Some ceased to exist.

The data on who got hurt is instructive. According to analyses of cryptocurrency market data, between 73% and 81% of crypto investors lost money between 2015 and 2022. The profits were disproportionately captured by early adopters, miners, and institutional players — not the retail FOMO buyers who arrived during peak media coverage.

## "Different This Time" Versus "Same as Every Time"

Every bubble generates the argument that this one is genuinely different from previous bubbles. The dotcom bulls argued that traditional valuation metrics didn't apply to internet companies. The crypto bulls argued that Bitcoin was digital gold and traditional valuation frameworks were irrelevant. The meme stock bulls argued that short squeezes could sustain elevated prices indefinitely.

These arguments have a common structure: they identify something real and new about the asset (internet scalability; digital scarcity; short squeeze mechanics) and then extrapolate from that real insight to the conclusion that normal financial gravity has been suspended.

Normal financial gravity is never suspended. Asset prices eventually have to connect to underlying value. When they disconnect too far, the reconnection is called a crash.

The investors who build sustainable wealth are not the ones who find the next revolutionary asset. Most revolutionary assets don't outperform boring index funds over 20-year periods. Amazon is the exception, not the rule, and even Amazon fell 92% during the dotcom crash before its eventual ascent. The investors who find the one Amazon for every thousand Pets.com companies they sifted through don't describe their journey as "easy money" — they describe it as decades of research, enormous portfolio volatility, and more than a little luck.

The investors who build sustainable wealth are, somewhat anticlimactically, the ones who owned the whole market in low-cost index funds and didn't trade. They owned Amazon and Apple and Google as they grew. They also owned the companies that went bankrupt, but those were replaced by new companies, and the winners compounded to far outweigh the losers.

## The r/personalfinance Postmortem

The r/personalfinance subreddit contains a particularly rich archive of the aftermath of every speculative mania. The posts arrive in waves: first the cautionary posts during the run-up ("don't put money in you can't afford to lose"), then the hopeful posts at the peak ("is now a good time to get in?"), then the desperate posts during the decline ("down 70%, should I sell or hold?"), and finally the reckoning posts a year or two later ("I took out a loan for crypto in 2021. I'm still paying it back.").

One r/personalfinance thread from 2022 described a user who had liquidated their emergency fund and a significant portion of their 401(k) to buy crypto near the peak, convinced that missing the crypto revolution was the worst financial mistake they could make. They were wrong. Missing the crypto collapse was the better outcome.

Another thread described a user who had borrowed $50,000 against their home equity to invest in meme stocks in early 2021 — not because they had done deep fundamental analysis, but because everyone around them seemed to be making money and they didn't want to be left behind.

These are not stupid people. They are human beings responding to social and psychological pressures that are extraordinarily powerful, in a media environment specifically engineered to amplify those pressures.

## The Bottom Line

The eternal search for easy money is eternal precisely because the psychological forces that drive it — fear of missing out, narrative seduction, social proof, the hope for a shortcut around decades of disciplined saving — are hardwired into human cognition. Every generation creates its own version of tulip mania. The flowers become internet companies, then houses, then cryptocurrencies, then meme stocks, then whatever comes next.

The protection against every iteration of this cycle is the same: build wealth slowly through systematic investment in diversified index funds, ignore the narratives, and remember that every time someone says this opportunity is once-in-a-generation, so is the loss that follows for the people who arrived at the peak.
