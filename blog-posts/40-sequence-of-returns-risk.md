# Sequence of Returns Risk: The Retirement Danger Nobody Warns You About

You've done everything right. You've spent 35 years buying index funds, ignoring the noise, and watching your portfolio grow. You hit your number. You retire. You've won.

Then the market drops 35% in your first year of retirement.

For an investor still in the accumulation phase, this is painful but recoverable. You have decades of future contributions to buy cheap shares and ride the recovery. Your portfolio bounces back and you eventually reach a higher high.

For someone who just retired and started withdrawing, this same drop can be catastrophic — even if the average annual return over the next 30 years ends up being perfectly fine. This is sequence of returns risk, and it's one of the most important — and least-discussed — threats to retirement security.

## Why the Order of Returns Matters More Than the Average

Imagine two investors. Both start retirement with $1,000,000. Both plan to withdraw $50,000 per year (the 5% withdrawal rate — aggressive, but we're using it for illustration). Both experience an average annual return of roughly 5% over 20 years.

The difference: **when** the bad years occur.

**Investor A** gets hit by two consecutive 15% down years in year one and year two, then enjoys 6% returns for the remaining 18 years.

**Investor B** enjoys 6% returns for the first nine years, then experiences two consecutive 15% down years, then recovers with 6% returns.

The average return for both investors is nearly identical. But [according to Charles Schwab research](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/timing-matters-understanding-sequence-returns-risk), Investor A — who faced the losses early — runs out of money far sooner than Investor B, who faced the same losses later.

Why? Because Investor A had to sell depressed shares to fund withdrawals during those down years. Every dollar withdrawn during a market drop removes shares that never get to participate in the eventual recovery. When the market bounces back, there are fewer shares to bounce with.

Investor B, on the other hand, spent nine years withdrawing at full or rising portfolio values. When the downturn hit, her portfolio was large enough and she had enough runway that the loss was absorbed without depleting the principal.

The math is striking. [A 2022 Morningstar study cited by CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/03/20/retirees-sequence-of-returns-risk.html) found that if a retiree's portfolio experiences a 15% decline in their first year of retirement coupled with a 3.3% withdrawal, their likelihood of running out of money within 30 years could be **six times greater** than that of someone who enjoyed a positive return in their first year. Six times greater. From a single bad year of timing.

## The Accumulation Phase vs. The Distribution Phase

During your working years (accumulation phase), sequence of returns works *in your favor* through dollar-cost averaging. When markets drop, your ongoing contributions buy more shares at lower prices. The recovery makes those cheap shares enormously valuable. Bad markets early in your investing career are actually good for your long-term outcome.

The distribution phase is the mirror image. You're not buying shares — you're selling them. When prices are down, you have to sell more shares to raise the same dollar amount for living expenses. And those shares are gone permanently. They can't recover.

This asymmetry is why financial planning that makes intuitive sense during accumulation — "just ride it out, the market always recovers" — needs adjustment at and near retirement. The exact same market drop has opposite implications depending on which phase you're in.

The first 5–10 years of retirement are the highest-risk window, sometimes called the "red zone" or "danger zone" by retirement researchers. [Amy Arnott, portfolio strategist at Morningstar](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/03/20/retirees-sequence-of-returns-risk.html), identifies the first five years as especially critical: withdrawals during declining markets lock in losses that the portfolio can never fully recover from at a meaningful scale.

## Strategies to Protect Against Sequence Risk

The goal isn't to eliminate market risk — it's to avoid being forced to sell equities at depressed prices during early retirement. Several strategies can help.

**1. The Bond Tent (or Glidepath Reversal)**

The traditional advice is to reduce equity exposure as you approach retirement — shifting from 80% stocks / 20% bonds to something like 50/50 or 40/60. The bond tent takes this further and specifically argues for *increasing* bond allocation temporarily around retirement, then slowly reducing it as you move through the danger zone.

The logic: bonds provide a buffer of stable assets to draw from during a stock market downturn. Instead of selling your beaten-down stocks to fund living expenses, you draw from bonds and short-term safe assets, giving your equities time to recover. Once 5–10 years into retirement have passed successfully, you gradually shift back toward equities.

This temporarily reduces long-term expected return, but also reduces the probability of catastrophic early sequence risk. It's insurance with a known cost.

**2. The Cash Bucket Strategy**

Keep 1–2 years of living expenses in cash or money market accounts, separate from your investment portfolio. In a market downturn, draw from the cash bucket rather than selling investments. This gives the portfolio time to recover without selling at the bottom.

Replenish the cash bucket during good market years. During bear markets, let it draw down rather than liquidating equities.

**3. Flexible Withdrawal Rules**

The rigid 4% rule (withdraw exactly 4%, adjusted for inflation, every year) works well in average conditions but amplifies sequence risk in bad markets. More flexible approaches:
- **Guardrails method:** Reduce withdrawals by 10% if the portfolio drops to a certain level; restore them when it recovers
- **Variable percentage withdrawal:** Withdraw a fixed percentage of the *current* portfolio value each year rather than a fixed dollar amount — naturally reducing withdrawals during down years
- **Floor and upside approach:** Fund guaranteed baseline expenses with annuities or bonds; allow the equity portion to vary

**4. Work a Little Longer — or Part-Time**

One of the most effective sequence-risk mitigations is retiring slightly later — or continuing part-time work early in retirement. Each additional year of work:
- Adds contributions to the portfolio
- Reduces the years the portfolio must self-sustain
- Delays when withdrawals begin, letting compounding continue
- Potentially allows you to draw Social Security at a higher rate

Even working part-time for two to three years post-"retirement" — covering basic expenses without drawing from the portfolio — dramatically reduces sequence risk by letting early equity volatility pass without portfolio withdrawals.

**5. Social Security Timing**

Delaying Social Security from age 62 to 70 increases your monthly benefit by approximately 6–8% per year. A retiree who delays to age 70 receives roughly 76% more per month than one who claims at 62. Every dollar of guaranteed Social Security income is a dollar you don't have to withdraw from your portfolio during market downturns. High Social Security income essentially provides a partial annuity that buffers sequence risk.

## A Practical Allocation Example as You Approach Retirement

Here's how a simple, phased approach might look for someone targeting a 65-year retirement:

**Ages 55–60 (approaching):** Begin shifting from 90/10 to ~70/30 equity/bond. Build the cash bucket. Review Social Security claiming strategy.

**Age 60–65 (the bond tent peak):** Target something like 50/50 or even 40/60. Maintain the cash bucket (1–2 years of expenses in short-term safe assets). This is temporarily more conservative than optimal — that's by design.

**Ages 65–75 (early retirement, danger zone):** Draw from bonds and cash bucket first during market downturns. Avoid selling equities if possible. Let the equity portion recover.

**Ages 75+ (later retirement):** Gradually increase equity exposure again (counter-intuitive, but the danger zone has passed). At this stage, a shorter time horizon reduces the impact of sequence risk, and higher equity exposure provides inflation protection and growth.

## The Bottom Line

Sequence of returns risk is retirement's hidden killer. Two investors with identical average returns and identical portfolios can have vastly different outcomes based on nothing more than the timing of market downturns relative to when they start withdrawing.

The key insights:
- Early retirement losses are disproportionately damaging because they force sales of depressed assets
- The first 5–10 years of retirement are the highest-risk window — the "danger zone"
- Strategies to manage this include: bond tent allocation, cash bucket, flexible withdrawal rules, part-time income, and delayed Social Security
- Don't just model average returns — consider *sequence* scenarios when stress-testing retirement plans

The accumulation phase rewards patience and riding volatility. The distribution phase demands protection from it. These are fundamentally different financial challenges — and the best investors treat them that way.
