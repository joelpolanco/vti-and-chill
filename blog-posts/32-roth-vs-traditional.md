# Roth vs. Traditional: The Great Retirement Account Debate

If you've spent more than five minutes on personal finance forums, you've seen it: the Roth vs. Traditional debate. People argue about it with the intensity normally reserved for pineapple on pizza or which "Star Wars" trilogy is best. Everyone has an opinion, most people are half right, and almost nobody explains it clearly.

So let's fix that.

This isn't a debate with a universal winner. It's a framework for understanding your situation and making the smart call. Once you understand how the tax math actually works, the right answer becomes much clearer for your specific circumstances.

## The Core Difference: When You Pay the Taxes

Both Roth and Traditional accounts give you tax advantages on retirement savings. The difference is *when* you pay.

**Traditional (pre-tax):** You contribute dollars before they've been taxed. If you earn $80,000 and contribute $10,000 to a traditional 401(k) or IRA, your taxable income drops to $70,000. You pay tax on the money when you withdraw it in retirement.

**Roth (post-tax):** You contribute dollars that have already been taxed. No deduction now. But when you withdraw in retirement — including all the growth — it's completely tax-free.

Same $80,000 salary, $10,000 to a Roth 401(k): you still pay taxes on $80,000 now. But in 30 years, when that $10,000 has become $76,000 at a 7% annual return, every dollar of it comes out tax-free.

The critical question is: **Will your tax rate be higher now or in retirement?**

- If you're in a higher bracket now than you'll be in retirement → **Traditional** wins. You defer taxes from a high rate to a lower rate.
- If you're in a lower bracket now than you'll be in retirement → **Roth** wins. You pay taxes now at the low rate and withdraw tax-free later.
- If your bracket is roughly the same → They're mathematically equivalent (assuming consistent rates), but Roth has some behavioral and flexibility advantages.

## Current Contribution Limits

For 2026, the contribution limits are:

- **401(k), 403(b), 457 plans:** $24,500 per year ($32,500 if you're 50 or older; $35,750 for ages 60–63 under SECURE 2.0 super catch-up rules)
- **IRA (Traditional or Roth):** $7,500 per year ($8,600 if you're 50 or older)

These limits typically adjust annually for inflation, so check IRS.gov for the most current figures.

**Roth IRA income limits for 2026:**
- Single filers: full contribution if MAGI is below $153,000; phases out completely at $168,000
- Married filing jointly: full contribution below $242,000; phases out at $252,000

Note that Roth *401(k)*s have no income limits. If your employer offers a Roth 401(k) option, anyone can contribute regardless of income — which is a significant advantage for high earners.

Traditional IRA *deductibility* also has limits if you (or your spouse) have a workplace retirement plan — it phases out at income levels that change annually. You can always make a non-deductible Traditional IRA contribution regardless of income, which is the foundation of the Backdoor Roth strategy (covered in a separate post).

## The Real-World Arguments for Each Side

**The case for Roth:**

1. **Tax diversification.** Nobody knows future tax rates with certainty. Having money in both taxable, Roth (tax-free), and Traditional (tax-deferred) buckets gives you flexibility to manage your tax bill in retirement.

2. **No Required Minimum Distributions.** Traditional accounts force you to start withdrawing at age 73 (under current SECURE 2.0 rules), whether you need the money or not, potentially pushing you into higher brackets. Roth IRAs have no RMDs during the account owner's lifetime.

3. **Young investors in low brackets.** If you're 25 and in the 22% bracket (or lower), your tax rate will likely be higher when you're peak-earning in your 40s or during retirement RMDs. Paying taxes now at 22% to avoid paying at 32% later is excellent math.

4. **The growth is enormous.** The Roth shields *all* of the compounded growth, not just the contribution. If you contribute $7,500 at age 25 and it grows to $120,000 by retirement at 65, that entire $120,000 comes out tax-free. The Traditional saves you taxes on $7,500 today. The Roth saves you taxes on $120,000 later.

5. **Flexibility.** Roth IRA *contributions* (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time, penalty-free, before retirement. This makes a Roth IRA a useful emergency backup — not a strategy to rely on, but good to know.

**The case for Traditional:**

1. **You're in a high bracket now.** If you're earning $250,000 and in the 32% or 35% federal bracket, the immediate tax deduction is powerful. Taking that deduction now and paying taxes at potentially lower rates in retirement is smart math.

2. **State taxes complicate things.** If you live in a high-income-tax state now but plan to retire in a no-income-tax state (Florida, Texas, Nevada, etc.), Traditional contributions get even better — you avoid state taxes entirely on that money.

3. **You need the cash flow.** The traditional contribution lowers your taxable income and reduces your current tax bill. If cash flow is tight and that deduction helps you afford the contribution, Traditional may be the practical choice.

4. **Employer match math.** Employer matches always go into traditional pre-tax accounts regardless of whether you choose Roth or Traditional for your own contributions. You're always getting some traditional exposure from matches.

**The Roth Conversion Ladder — A Third Strategy**

One powerful play: contribute to Traditional accounts now (for the deduction), retire early, and systematically convert Traditional funds to Roth during low-income years. Since you're no longer earning a salary, your taxable income is low — meaning you convert at 12% or 22% instead of 32% or 37%.

This "Roth conversion ladder" is a cornerstone of early retirement strategies. You pay taxes during your low-income years to fill up the lower brackets, creating a large pool of tax-free Roth money for the rest of your life.

The catch: Roth conversions are taxable in the year you do them, and converted funds need to sit for five years before being withdrawn penalty-free (the "5-year rule"). Planning matters here.

## A Practical Framework

If you're trying to make a decision right now, here's a reasonable starting point:

- **Under 30 or in the 22% bracket or below:** Lean Roth. You have decades for compounding and likely face higher rates ahead.
- **In the 32% bracket or higher:** Lean Traditional, especially if you expect lower income in retirement. Use a Roth 401(k) if you want some Roth exposure regardless of income.
- **In the 24% bracket:** Classic "hedge your bets" territory — contribute to both. Max the Traditional 401(k) for the deduction, but also fund a Roth IRA for flexibility and tax diversification.
- **Expect very high retirement income (large portfolio, rental income, business interests):** Roth conversions and Roth contributions now protect from what could be very high RMD-driven income later.

And if anyone tells you there's one universal right answer? They're selling something.

## The Bottom Line

Roth and Traditional are tools, not religions. The Roth wins if you're paying lower taxes now than you will in retirement. Traditional wins if you're paying higher taxes now. When in doubt, split the difference — contribute to both to give yourself options.

The most important variable isn't Roth vs. Traditional. It's whether you're maxing out your tax-advantaged accounts at all. A fully-funded Traditional IRA beats a half-funded Roth IRA every time. Get the money invested. Then optimize.

Start with your employer's 401(k) up to the match (free money, always take it). Then fund a Roth IRA if you're eligible. Then go back and max the 401(k). In that order, regardless of Roth vs. Traditional nuances, you're building serious long-term wealth.
