# The Market Always Goes Up (But It's Never a Smooth Ride)

In March 2020, the stock market dropped about 34% in 33 days. It was the fastest bear market in history. COVID-19 was spreading globally, entire economies were shutting down, and nobody had any idea how long it would last or how bad it would get.

If you had invested $100,000 at the beginning of 2020 and watched it become $66,000 by mid-March, what would you have done?

A lot of people sold. They locked in their losses, moved to cash, and planned to wait until things "got better." And then they watched, stunned, as the market fully recovered in about five months — one of the fastest recoveries ever recorded — and went on to post remarkable gains by year's end.

The lesson isn't that markets always recover quickly. Sometimes they take years. The lesson is that they always recover. And then they keep going.

---

## The Long View Changes Everything

The human brain is not wired for long-term thinking. We're wired for threat detection. When the market falls 10%, your amygdala doesn't say "excellent, time to buy at a discount." It says "danger — run." This is a feature, not a bug, if you're trying to avoid predators on the savanna. It is a catastrophic bug if you're trying to build wealth over 30 years.

To rewire your instincts, you need to zoom out.

The [S&P 500 has delivered an average annual return of approximately 10% over the last 100 years](https://tradethatswing.com/average-historical-stock-market-returns-for-sp-500-5-year-up-to-150-year-averages/), with dividends reinvested. That's the number that matters. Not what it does this week. Not what some analyst predicted this quarter. That 10% average is the engine humming under all the noise.

But here's what that number disguises: the ride to that 10% average is deeply, profoundly bumpy.

In the 20th century alone, the U.S. stock market survived two World Wars, the Great Depression (which saw stocks fall roughly 89% from peak to trough), a dozen recessions, the Cold War, the assassination of a sitting president, the oil embargoes of the 1970s, double-digit inflation, a dot-com bubble that wiped out trillions, and the 2008 financial crisis that came within days of collapsing the global banking system. And after every single one of those events, the market eventually recovered and reached new highs.

Every single time.

Charlie Munger, Warren Buffett's long-time partner at Berkshire Hathaway, framed the investor's challenge this way: "It's not supposed to be easy. Anyone who finds it easy is stupid." The discomfort is the price. Market volatility is not a design flaw in capitalism — it's the mechanism by which patient investors earn their premium over risk-free assets. The temporary pain is what makes the long-run reward possible.

---

## Every Crisis Looked Unsurvivable From the Inside

This is the part they don't teach you in financial journalism, because financial journalism covers the present, not the past.

When the market crashed in 1929, thoughtful people genuinely believed capitalism might be over. When it crashed in 1987 (down 22% in a single day — still the largest single-day percentage drop ever recorded), it felt like the system was broken. In 2008, Ben Bernanke and Hank Paulson were in rooms briefing Congressional leaders that if they didn't act immediately, ATMs might stop dispensing cash by Monday morning. In March 2020, epidemiologists were modeling scenarios where 60% of the global population would get infected.

And yet. Here we are.

The pattern is consistent: a crisis arrives, it looks catastrophic, fear peaks, the market bottoms, and then it climbs. The bottom never announced itself in advance. There was no news alert that said "worst point reached, safe to buy now." There was only chaos — and then, in retrospect, an obvious opportunity.

This is why the buy-and-hold investor wins. Not because they're smarter about predicting recoveries. But because they're *still invested* when the recovery happens. And recoveries, historically, are fast and violent. Miss the early stages and you miss a disproportionate share of the gains.

Consider: according to data from Hartford Funds, [if you missed the market's 10 best days over a 30-year period, your returns would have been cut in half](https://www.hartfordfunds.com/practice-management/client-conversations/managing-volatility/timing-the-market-is-impossible.html). Many of those best days happened during or immediately after crashes — the exact moments when panic sellers were sitting in cash waiting for things to "calm down."

## What "The Market Always Goes Up" Actually Means

A caveat worth stating clearly: "the market always goes up" is a historical observation about long time horizons, not a guarantee about any specific period. Japan's Nikkei 225 index peaked in 1989 and still hasn't matched that peak — more than 35 years later. Individual stocks can and do go to zero. Specific sectors get disrupted and never recover.

This is exactly why owning a diversified total market index fund, rather than individual stocks or narrow sector funds, is such a powerful structure. When you own VTI, you own roughly 3,500+ companies across every sector of the U.S. economy. When one industry crumbles, another rises. The index itself is self-cleansing — companies that fail get replaced by companies that succeed. The composition in 2050 will look nothing like 2025, and that's precisely the point.

The broader principle — that diversified ownership of productive assets in a growing economy trends upward over decades — has held across virtually every major developed market over every long time period we have data for. Will it hold in the future? Nobody can promise that. But the burden of proof is on the person betting against human ingenuity, economic growth, and the long-run expansion of productive capacity.

That's a bet we'll pass on.

JL Collins put it memorably: "The market always goes up. Not always, not every year, not every decade — but always over time. And that simple truth is all you need."

## The Investor's Edge: Optimism Is Rational

Here's a reframe that might change how you think about investing during scary moments. Pessimism about the stock market — the belief that this crash is different, that the system is broken, that recovery isn't coming — requires you to believe that human beings have permanently stopped being productive, creative, and economically active. That companies have stopped finding better ways to deliver goods and services. That technological progress has halted. That global trade has ended.

Historically, that belief has been wrong every single time.

Optimism about the market's long-run direction isn't naive — it's an evidence-based bet on the same force that has driven economic growth for centuries: human ingenuity compounding over time. When you own a total market index fund, you own a diversified stake in American (and global) productive capacity. When the economy grows, you grow with it. When new industries emerge, they become part of your holdings. When old industries fade, they shrink as a percentage of your portfolio rather than disappearing from it entirely.

Buffett's annual letter to Berkshire shareholders has made this point repeatedly: never bet against America. Not as patriotism, but as probability. The U.S. has the rule of law, deep capital markets, a culture of entrepreneurship, and institutions (however imperfect) that protect property rights and contract enforcement. These are the structural conditions for long-run economic growth. They have been present for 100+ years of stock market history, and they produced the 10% annualized returns we keep citing.

You don't need to predict the next crisis. You don't need to time the recovery. You just need to be there, consistently, through all of it.

---

## The Bottom Line

The stock market has survived every war, every recession, every panic, every scandal, and every black swan event in modern history — and it has made patient long-term investors wealthy along the way. The bumps are real, the crashes are painful, and the urge to flee when things get scary is completely understandable. But the historical record is clear: time in the market beats timing the market, recoveries are inevitable for diversified investors, and the cost of selling at the wrong moment is enormous. The market always goes up. Your only job is to still be there when it does.
